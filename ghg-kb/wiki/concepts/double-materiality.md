## Definition

Double materiality is the principle that a company's sustainability reporting must assess and disclose information from **two directions simultaneously**:

1. **Impact materiality (Inside-Out):** The company's actual and potential impacts — positive and negative — on people and the environment across its operations and upstream/downstream value chain. Includes Scope 1, 2, and 3 emission sources.

2. **Financial materiality (Outside-In):** The sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could have a material effect on the company's financial position, financial performance, cash flows, access to finance, or cost of capital over short-, medium-, or long-term horizons.

A topic may be material from one or both directions. If material from **either** direction, it must be reported. The two dimensions are inter-related — an impact may also be financial, an impact may become financially material over time — and the assessment generally **starts from impact** before testing financial materiality (ESRS 1 paragraph 38).

Double materiality is the cornerstone of CSRD reporting scope. Under [[regulations/esrs-1|ESRS 1]] (General Requirements), every in-scope undertaking must conduct a **Double Materiality Assessment (DMA)** before determining which ESRS standards and disclosure requirements apply.

(→ [[sources/esrs-set1-2023-2772|ESRS Set 1 — Commission Delegated Regulation 2023/2772]]) (→ [[sources/csrd-directive-2022-2464|CSRD Directive]])

## Why It Matters

Double materiality determines **what a company must actually report** under CSRD. A company that concludes a topic is non-material from both directions may omit its disclosures entirely.

**Special climate rule (ESRS 1 paragraph 32):** climate change is the *only* topic where non-materiality requires a **detailed forward-looking justification**, including conditions that could lead climate to become material in the future. This justification is reported under [[regulations/esrs-2|ESRS 2 IRO-2]]. For all other topics, a brief explanation suffices. The rule reflects the regulatory presumption that climate is the de-facto baseline material topic.

**E1-1 transition plan exception:** even if climate is assessed as non-material, ESRS E1-1 transition plan disclosure still applies if a transition plan exists.

In practice, climate change is expected to be material for nearly all in-scope companies for at least one direction:
- The company has Scope 1, 2, or 3 emissions that represent impacts on the climate (impact materiality), or
- Physical or transition risks from climate change affect the company's financial outlook (financial materiality)

Double materiality differs from the **single financial materiality** standard used by US disclosure frameworks (SEC) and global frameworks (ISSB, IFRS S1/S2). The EU explicitly chose double materiality to capture impact on society regardless of whether it affects the reporting company's finances.

## Key Distinctions

### Double materiality vs. financial materiality (ISSB / TCFD / SEC)

- **ISSB / IFRS S2 (global baseline):** financial materiality only — disclose what affects investors
- **CSRD / ESRS:** double materiality — disclose impacts on the world AND what affects investors
- A pollution event that harms local communities but doesn't affect company finances is material under ESRS, not under ISSB

### Impact materiality — assessment criteria (ESRS 1 paragraphs 43–46)

A sustainability matter is material from an impact perspective when it pertains to actual or potential, positive or negative impacts on people or the environment over short-, medium-, or long-term — including impacts connected through products, services, and business relationships in the upstream and downstream value chain.

For **negative impacts**, materiality is informed by the due diligence process under the **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights** and the **OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises**. Severity is the determining factor:

- **Scale** of the impact
- **Scope** of the impact
- **Irremediable character** of the impact

For **actual** negative impacts: severity alone determines materiality.
For **potential** negative impacts: severity AND likelihood.
For **potential negative human rights impacts**: severity takes precedence over likelihood.

For **positive impacts**:
- For actual impacts: scale and scope
- For potential impacts: scale, scope, and likelihood

### Financial materiality — assessment criteria (ESRS 1 paragraphs 47–51)

A matter is material from a financial perspective if it triggers, or could reasonably be expected to trigger, **material financial effects** on the undertaking — material influence on:
- Development
- Financial position
- Financial performance
- Cash flows
- Access to finance
- Cost of capital

Over short-, medium-, or long-term horizons. Risks and opportunities may derive from past or future events. Financial materiality extends beyond the financial-statement consolidation perimeter — it includes material risks/opportunities attributable to **business relationships beyond consolidation**.

**Dependencies on resources** (natural, human, social) are explicit sources of financial materiality:
- They may influence the undertaking's ability to obtain or use needed resources, and the quality/pricing of those resources
- They may affect the ability to rely on relationships needed in business processes on acceptable terms

Materiality is assessed via **likelihood of occurrence × magnitude of financial effects**.

### Materiality of information (ESRS 1 paragraphs 31–36)

Distinct from materiality of *matters*. Within a material matter, an undertaking discloses datapoints if the information is:
- **Significant** in relation to the matter it depicts, or
- Capable of meeting users' decision-making needs (financial users + impact users)

For metrics, an undertaking **may omit** a datapoint of a material Disclosure Requirement if the information is itself not material, except where the datapoint derives from other EU legislation listed in ESRS 2 Appendix B (in which case the omission must be explicitly stated as "not material").

### Materiality thresholds

ESRS 1 does **not** set a single quantitative threshold. The undertaking shall:
- Establish appropriate thresholds, qualitative and/or quantitative
- Disclose how those thresholds are applied (under IRO-1)
- Use thresholds consistently across the materiality assessment and reporting

This judgment must be informed by stakeholder engagement, scenario analysis, peer benchmarking, and internal expert review.

**Threshold-setting practice (per EFRAG IG 1 §3.6, §3.7):**
- **Impact materiality** thresholds typically combine **severity bands** (low / moderate / high / very high) with **likelihood bands** (rare / possible / likely / very likely) into a 4×4 or 5×5 matrix; impacts above the diagonal threshold are material. IG 1 Figures 4–5 illustrate the construction.
- **Financial materiality** thresholds may be absolute (e.g. EUR 5M effect over 5 years) or relative (e.g. ≥5% of revenue, ≥3% of operating cash flow, ≥10% of equity). IG 1 ¶133–135 makes clear that effects **not yet on the balance sheet** — dependencies on natural / human / social resources, reputational risks affecting cost of capital — also count toward financial materiality.
- The full process (four steps, threshold mechanics, value-chain extension, stakeholder engagement, worked example) is captured in [[methodologies/double-materiality-assessment|Double Materiality Assessment]].

(→ [[sources/efrag-ig1-materiality-2024|EFRAG IG 1 — Materiality (May 2024)]])

### Material IROs from actions (ESRS 1 paragraphs 52–53)

Importantly, the materiality assessment may identify situations where actions taken to address one matter create material negative impacts or risks for another. Example: a decarbonisation plan that involves abandoning certain product lines may create material workforce-related risks via redundancy. Such intersectional impacts must be disclosed.

## Calculation Notes

The DMA process does not involve GHG calculations directly, but it determines which emission-related disclosures are required. EFRAG's IG 1 §3 recommends a four-step structured process — Step A (understand context) → Step B (identify long-list IROs) → Step C (assess and set thresholds) → Step D (report) — applied to **own operations and the value chain together**. The full step-by-step procedure, severity-likelihood matrix construction, value-chain boundary mechanics, tiered data approach, and worked example are codified in [[methodologies/double-materiality-assessment|Double Materiality Assessment]].

For climate (ESRS E1): if material, all nine E1 DRs apply (subject to [[concepts/esrs-phase-in|phase-in]]). If immaterial, the **detailed forward-looking justification** described above is required, and E1-1 transition plan still applies if one exists.

**Stakeholder engagement (IG 1 §3.5):** ESRS does not mandate a specific engagement method but **disclosure of whether and how engagement occurred is required** (IRO-1). Affected stakeholders inform impact materiality; users of sustainability statements (investors, lenders, business partners) inform financial materiality. Engagement may piggy-back on existing business processes (HR surveys, supplier audits, customer NPS, investor day Q&A). Silent stakeholders (the environment, future generations) are represented through proxies — scientific evidence, expert NGOs, regulatory frameworks.
