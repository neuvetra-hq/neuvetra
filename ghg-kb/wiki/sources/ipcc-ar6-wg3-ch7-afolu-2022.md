## Key Takeaways

1. **AFOLU = 13–21% of global anthropogenic GHG emissions (2010–2019, medium confidence).** Net anthropogenic CO2 from AFOLU was +5.9 ± 4.1 GtCO2/yr per book-keeping models — but only 0.0 to +0.8 GtCO2/yr per FAOSTAT and national GHG inventories (NGHGI). Agricultural CH4 (157 ± 47 Mt/yr → 4.2 ± 1.3 GtCO2-eq/yr at AR6 GWP100) is dominated by enteric fermentation; agricultural N2O (6.6 ± 4.0 Mt/yr → 1.8 ± 1.1 GtCO2-eq/yr) is dominated by manure application, atmospheric N deposition, and synthetic N fertiliser.

2. **The 5.5 GtCO2/yr methodological discrepancy is material for offset credibility.** NGHGI (IPCC-guidelines-based) and global book-keeping models disagree on the boundary between "anthropogenic" and "natural" forest fluxes, and on which forests count as "managed land". Cross-Chapter Box 6 documents the gap: NGHGI consider human-induced environmental-change effects on managed forests to be anthropogenic and report them; book-keeping models exclude them. Reconciliation is required for credible AFOLU-based offsetting and accurate Global Stocktake. (high confidence)

3. **AFOLU mitigation potential 2020–2050: 8–14 GtCO2-eq/yr at <USD100/tCO2-eq.** This is roughly half the technical potential. About 30–50% of the economic potential is achievable below USD20/tCO2-eq. Breakdown by lever:
    - **Forests + other natural ecosystems: 7.3 (3.9–13.1) GtCO2-eq/yr** — protection, improved management, restoration of forests, peatlands, coastal wetlands, savannas, grasslands.
    - **Agriculture: 4.1 (1.7–6.7) GtCO2-eq/yr** — cropland and grassland soil-carbon management, agroforestry, biochar, improved rice cultivation, livestock and nutrient management.
    - **Demand-side: 2.2 (1.1–3.6) GtCO2-eq/yr** — sustainable healthy diets, food-waste reduction, building with wood and bio-based materials.

4. **BECCS technical potential by 2050: 5.9 (0.5–11.3) GtCO2/yr** considering only the technical capture of CO2 and underground storage. Of this, **1.6 (0.8–3.5) GtCO2/yr is available below USD100/tCO2-eq** (medium confidence). Bioenergy technical potential ranges 5–50 EJ/yr for residues and 50–250 EJ/yr for dedicated biomass production by 2050.

5. **AFOLU has under-delivered historically.** Past policy has produced ~0.65 GtCO2/yr of AFOLU mitigation during 2010–2019 — only 1.4% of global gross emissions. Over 80% of historical AFOLU mitigation came from forestry measures (high confidence).

6. **Funding gap.** Estimated USD 0.7 billion/yr currently spent on AFOLU mitigation versus USD 400+ billion/yr needed to deliver the 30% of global mitigation envisaged in deep-mitigation scenarios (medium confidence). The required funding is *less* than current agricultural and forestry subsidies — redirection of existing subsidies is the highest-leverage policy lever.

7. **Land carbon sink absorbs ~30% of anthropogenic CO2 emissions.** Combined natural + managed terrestrial ecosystems are a gross sink of −12.5 ± 3.2 GtCO2/yr (2010–2019). When this sink is netted against AFOLU sources, the land overall is a net sink of −6.6 ± 5.2 GtCO2/yr in CO2 terms (medium confidence) — but this nets natural sinks against anthropogenic emissions, not a meaningful basis for corporate or national accounting.

8. **Mitigation co-benefits and trade-offs are highly context-specific.** Forestry, peatland, agroforestry, soil-carbon, livestock-management measures generally deliver biodiversity, water, air-quality, food-security co-benefits — when designed for local conditions. Poorly planned biomass and afforestation can conflict with biodiversity and food security. "Nature-Based Solutions" framing emphasizes co-benefit-positive measures.

9. **CH4 mitigation is the agricultural priority.** Enteric fermentation from ruminants is the largest agricultural CH4 source. Emerging technologies (vaccines, methane inhibitors like 3-NOP, seaweed-based feed additives) have potential to substantially increase CH4 mitigation beyond current sectoral-study estimates. AR6 100-year GWP for CH4 is 27.9 (non-fossil) / 29.8 (fossil); for N2O is 273.

10. **Smaller countries can have disproportionately high AFOLU mitigation density.** Particularly those with significant peatlands and wetlands. AFOLU mitigation is not only a "large land-area country" issue — it is a dense, distributed potential.
